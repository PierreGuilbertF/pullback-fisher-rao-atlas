Pullback Fisher–Rao Geometries of Parametric Probability Families: An Expository Atlas

Pierre Guilbert · September 1, 2026 · Preprint

Abstract

We study the Fisher-Rao geometry induced on finite-dimensional statistical manifolds by pulling back the Fisher-Rao metric from the space of positive probability densities to the parameter space of a parametric probability model. Starting from this common construction, we derive and interpret the resulting Riemannian metrics for univariate and multivariate Gaussian distributions, elliptical Laplace distributions, linear and nonlinear Gibbs families, and probability families obtained as pushforwards of a fixed reference distribution. For each family, we compute the differential of the parameter-to-density map, derive the pullback metric tensor, and give a geometric interpretation of its components in terms of location, scale, anisotropy, orientation, energy fluctuations, or local mass compression. We then study the associated geodesics, using closed-form expressions when available and discrete Riemannian energy minimization otherwise. The purpose of this article is expository: to provide a unified geometric reading of several classical parametric probability models and to show how the same Fisher-Rao construction leads to distinct geometries on their parameter spaces. We finally contrast this viewpoint with Wasserstein-Otto geometry at the infinitesimal level.

1 Introduction

Geometry is fascinating in that it shapes intuition by giving form to mathematical objects. By making them visual, it allows us to look at them, deform them, measure them, or even traverse them, and thus to think about them differently. It is through this geometric lens that we propose to study certain probability distributions: parametric families then become geometric objects in their own right, on which distances can be measured and geodesics followed.

Let 𝒫+(E)\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) denote the set of strictly positive probability densities on a vector space EE, and let gFRg^{\mathrm{FR}} denote the Fisher–Rao form that will be written below. Pulling this form back along a family θ↦pθ\theta\mapsto p_{\theta} equips the parameter space with a Riemannian geometry.

Figure 1 gives a first glimpse. The top row shows three univariate densities, each corresponding to one point in a family. The bottom row shows the parameter plane: the colours and black curves measure a separation from the marked point. For the Gaussian family, this is the exact Fisher–Rao distance. For the two Gibbs families, it is the Riemannian length of the Euclidean chord, which is an upper bound on the geodesic distance.

In this paper, we first revisit the construction of the Fisher–Rao metric and the principle of pullback, before presenting an atlas of the geometries obtained for several familiar parametric families.

For each family, the scheme is the same: we specify the parameterization, show a few members, compute the differential of θ↦pθ\theta \mapsto p_{\theta} into 𝒫+(E)\mathcal{P}_{+}(E), derive the metric from it, and then analyze this metric and consider several geodesics. A reader more interested in intuition may skim the calculations and focus on the form of the metric, its interpretation, and the figures showing the geodesics.

Most of the individual ingredients considered below are classical when taken separately. The Fisher information geometry of Gaussian models has been studied extensively [11], [12], [14], [15], [16], general elliptical families were treated, for instance, by Mitchell [13], and the Hessian and dualistic geometry of exponential families is standard in information geometry [17], [18]. The relation between densities and diffeomorphism groups underlying the push-forward viewpoint is also part of the broader geometric picture developed in [6]. The purpose of the present article is not to introduce a new information metric, but to place these constructions within a single pullback framework and to compare, through explicit calculations and visualizations, how different parametrizations turn the same ambient Fisher–Rao metric into different finite-dimensional geometries. Particular emphasis is placed on the geometric interpretation of the metric components, on the comparison of the resulting geodesics, and on the push-forward formulation in terms of local mass compression.

2.1 Probability distributions

We work on the measure space (E,ℬ(E),λ)(E, \mathcal{B}(E), \lambda), where EE denotes a Euclidean space (most often ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}), ℬ(E)\mathcal{B}(E) the Borel σ\sigma-algebra associated with the topology induced by the inner product on EE, and λ\lambda the corresponding Lebesgue measure.

2.3.1 Topological manifold

2.3.4 Length, distance, geodesics

The Riemannian metric gg induces a notion of length for curves on ℳ\mathcal{M}. Let γ:[a,b]→ℳ\gamma \colon [a,b] \to \mathcal{M} be piecewise 𝒞1\mathcal{C}^{1}. Its length is

3 The Fisher–Rao metric

The name comes from two papers. Fisher [1] introduces the information carried by a parametric family in the form of an inner product on the scores ∂ilog⁡pθ\partial_{i}\log p_{\theta}. Rao [2] interprets this information as a Riemannian line element on the parameter space: this is the pullback that will be written below.

The distinguished role of this metric is also reflected in characterization results of Čencov type; see, for instance, Campbell [3].

The construction below embeds positive densities, viewed as elements of L1(E)L^{1}(E) (a vector space with no natural inner product), into L2(E)L^{2}(E) equipped with its Hilbert geometry ⟨f,g⟩L2=∫Efg\left\langle f,g \right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{E} f\,g. The map is the square-root map p↦2pp\mapsto 2\sqrt{p}: pulling back the inner product of L2L^{2} equips the densities with a geometry that is no longer Euclidean. On a finite alphabet, this embedding is a 𝒞∞\mathcal{C}^{\infty} immersion and the construction is rigorous. When EE is continuous, we retain the same formulas, restricting them to the directions for which they make sense.

3.2 Interpretation of the metric

For u∈Tp𝒫+(E)u \in T_{p}\mathcal{P}_{+}(E), the norm is ‖u‖gpFR2=gpFR(u,u)=∫Eu(x)2p(x)dx=𝔼x∼p[(u(x)p(x))2].\lVert u \rVert_{g^{\mathrm{FR}}_{p}}^{2} = g^{\mathrm{FR}}_{p}(u, u) = \int_{E} \frac{u(x)^{2}}{p(x)}\, dx = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\!\left[ \left(\frac{u(x)}{p(x)}\right)^{2} \right]. In other words, what matters is not so much the absolute amplitude of u(x)u(x) as the ratio u(x)/p(x)u(x)/p(x): the same change in density becomes more costly as p(x)p(x) becomes smaller. Modifying the distribution where it is already rare has a high cost; modifying it where it is concentrated costs much less.

A numerical example makes this immediate. Consider a local increment u(x)=0,05u(x) = 0{,}05. If p(x)=0,1p(x) = 0{,}1, its contribution to the integrand is u(x)2/p(x)=0,025u(x)^{2}/p(x) = 0{,}025. If p(x)=100p(x) = 100, it falls to 2,5⋅10−62{,}5 \cdot 10^{-6}. The absolute variation is the same, but the two costs are of entirely different orders.

This metric is also invariant under reparameterization of the observation space. If ϕ:E→E′\phi:E\to E' is a diffeomorphism and the densities are transported by ϕ\phi, the inner products gFRg^{\mathrm{FR}} remain unchanged: only the way in which the densities differ from one another matters, not the coordinate system chosen on EE. This invariance is in fact characteristic of the Fisher–Rao metric in several infinite-dimensional settings; see Bauer, Bruveris, and Michor [5].

It therefore differs from the Wasserstein metric, which arises from optimal transport [7]. The latter sees the points of EE through a spatial cost ‖x−y‖\lVert x - y \rVert; moving mass along EE has a cost that depends on the ambient geometry. Fisher–Rao, by contrast, measures a statistical difference: at the infinitesimal scale, the relative amplitude of density variations, that is, a local lack of overlap. Two distributions that are close in the Fisher–Rao sense may be far apart in the Wasserstein sense, and conversely, as soon as the geometry of EE comes into play.

3.4 A quick note on Natural gradient and steepest descent

There is one immediate consequence of equipping the parameter space with a metric. Let f:Θ→ℝf:\Theta\to\mathbb{R} be a cost function. As recalled in Section 2.2, its differential dfθdf_\theta is a linear form on TθΘT_\theta\Theta. A gradient, however, is a vector, and turning the linear form dfθdf_\theta into such a vector requires an inner product. With the usual Euclidean structure on ℝk\mathbb{R}^k, the Euclidean gradient ∇eucf(θ)\nabla_{\mathrm{euc}} f(\theta) is defined by dfθ(dθ)=⟨∇eucf(θ),dθ⟩.df_\theta(d\theta) = \left\langle \nabla_{\mathrm{euc}} f(\theta),d\theta \right\rangle . It therefore reflects not only ff, but also the Euclidean geometry chosen on the parameter coordinates.

Once Θ\Theta is equipped instead with the Riemannian metric gg, the same differential has another representative. We denote by ∇gf(θ)\nabla_g f(\theta) the unique tangent vector such that dfθ(dθ)=gθ(∇gf(θ),dθ),dθ∈TθΘ.df_\theta(d\theta) = g_\theta\!\left(\nabla_g f(\theta),d\theta\right), \qquad d\theta\in T_\theta\Theta . Writing the metric in coordinates as gθ(u,v)=u⊤G(θ)v,g_\theta(u,v)=u^\top G(\theta)v, gives immediately ∇gf(θ)=G(θ)−1∇eucf(θ).\nabla_g f(\theta) = G(\theta)^{-1}\nabla_{\mathrm{euc}} f(\theta). When gg is the Fisher–Rao metric pulled back from the space of densities, this Riemannian gradient is the natural gradient [22].

The factor G(θ)−1G(\theta)^{-1} has a simple geometric reading. Ordinary gradient descent regards all parameter directions as having the same local cost. The Riemannian metric replaces the Euclidean unit circle by dθ⊤G(θ)dθ=1.d\theta^\top G(\theta)d\theta=1. The direction of steepest descent is therefore no longer determined among Euclidean displacements of equal length, but among displacements of equal Riemannian length.

The natural gradient may therefore be viewed as the ordinary gradient after locally straightening the geometry of the parameter space. The function itself has not changed, but its graph expressed over these locally rescaled coordinates is stretched accordingly, and its direction of steepest variation changes with it.

A two-dimensional example makes the effect visible. Consider f(θ1,θ2)=12(θ12+θ22)f(\theta_1,\theta_2) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2\right) and the constant metric G=(2112).G= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1\\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. The level sets of ff are Euclidean circles, whereas the unit circles of gg are tilted ellipses. At θ=(1,0)\theta=(1,0), ∇eucf=(10),∇gf=G−1∇eucf=13(2−1).\nabla_{\mathrm{euc}} f = \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \nabla_g f = G^{-1}\nabla_{\mathrm{euc}} f = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2\\ -1 \end{pmatrix}. The two gradients therefore point in different directions. In coordinates that make the metric Euclidean, the second direction is again an ordinary steepest direction; its deviation in the θ\theta-plane comes from expressing this geometry in the original parameters.

For a varying metric G(θ)G(\theta), this interpretation is local: the metric can always be made Euclidean at one point by such a linear change of tangent coordinates, but a single reparameterization need not flatten it everywhere. A first-order Riemannian gradient descent step in the present coordinates reads θm+1=θm−αG(θm)−1∇eucf(θm).\theta_{m+1} = \theta_m - \alpha\, G(\theta_m)^{-1} \nabla_{\mathrm{euc}} f(\theta_m). For the families considered below, the matrix GG is precisely the Fisher–Rao metric derived from the variation of the density. Natural gradient descent therefore measures a step in parameter space by the statistical change it produces, rather than by the Euclidean size of the parameter increment.

4 Gaussian family

Let us begin with the most emblematic family: the Gaussian family. It plays a central role for two reasons. For a fixed variance, it maximizes entropy [10], and it appears as the limiting distribution in the central limit theorem.

4.1 Univariate Gaussian

4.1.1 Differential

4.1.3 Analysis of the metric

Up to a scaling factor, we recognize the metric of the Poincaré hyperbolic half-plane. The associated Riemannian distance then has a closed-form expression. Figure 11 shows this distance from the standard Gaussian (μ,σ)=(0,1)(\mu, \sigma) = (0, 1). Here we recover, on the parameter space, the interpretation from section 3.2. The metric is governed by the variance. Through the identification with the Poincaré half-plane, the geodesics are known explicitly: they are vertical rays (pure changes of σ\sigma at fixed μ\mu) or semicircles orthogonal to the axis σ=0\sigma = 0, after the change of coordinates ξ=μ/2\xi = \mu / \sqrt{2}. The metric is block-diagonal: gμ,Σ=(gμμ00gΣΣ),g_{\mu, \Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix} g^{\mu\mu} & 0 \\ 0 & g^{\Sigma\Sigma} \end{pmatrix}, which can also be written in differential form as ds2=dμ⊤Σ−1dμ+12tr⁡(Σ−1dΣΣ−1dΣ).ds^{2} = d\mu^{\top} \Sigma^{-1} d\mu + \frac{1}{2}\, \mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\!\bigl( \Sigma^{-1} d\Sigma\, \Sigma^{-1} d\Sigma \bigr).

4.2.3 Analysis of the metric

In higher dimensions, interpreting the metric requires a little more care. Its mechanisms can nevertheless be disentangled using the spectral decomposition of Σ\Sigma. Since Σ\Sigma is symmetric positive definite, the spectral theorem gives Σ=RDR⊤,R∈SO(n),D=diag⁡(σ12,…,σn2),\Sigma = R\, D\, R^{\top}, \qquad R \in \mathrm{SO}(n),\quad D = \mathop{\mathrm{diag}}(\sigma_{1}^{2}, \ldots, \sigma_{n}^{2}), with σi>0\sigma_{i} > 0: σi\sigma_{i} is the standard deviation along the ii-th column of RR, that is, along the ii-th principal axis of the Gaussian.

The spectral representation should be understood locally when it is used as a system of coordinates. When the eigenvalues are distinct, the principal axes can be chosen smoothly locally, up to the usual discrete ambiguities. When two eigenvalues coincide, however, the corresponding orientation is no longer determined by Σ\Sigma: rotations inside the associated eigenspace leave the covariance matrix unchanged. Thus the variables (σi,R)(\sigma_i,R) are used below primarily to interpret the different components of the metric, rather than as global coordinates on S++nS_{++}^n.

Three elementary motions then emerge: a shift of the mean at fixed covariance; a change in standard deviation along one or more directions, with the orientation and mean held fixed; and finally a rotation of these axes, with the standard deviations and mean held fixed. We illustrate these three motions using bivariate Gaussians, where the dimension remains low enough for the eye to follow. This parametrization by eigenvalues, orientation, and mean is the one used by Costa, Santos, and Strapasson [14] in the bivariate case.

To make this interpretation explicit, we express the metric gg in terms of dD∈TDDiag++(n)=Diag(n)dD \in T_{D}\mathrm{Diag}_{++}(n) = \mathrm{Diag}(n) and dR∈TRSO(n)={RΩ:Ω⊤=−Ω}dR \in T_{R}\mathrm{SO}(n) = \{ R\Omega : \Omega^{\top} = -\Omega \}. We then have dΣ=RdDR⊤+dRDR⊤+RDdR⊤.d\Sigma = R\, dD\, R^{\top} + dR\, D\, R^{\top} + R\, D\, dR^{\top}. Expressing the variation of the mean in the basis that diagonalizes the covariance, η=R⊤dμ\eta = R^{\top} d\mu, gives ds2=∑i=1nηi2σi2+2∑i=1ndσi2σi2+∑1≤i<j≤n(σi2−σj2)2σi2σj2Ωij2\boxed{\, ds^{2} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\eta_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}} + 2\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{d\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}} + \sum_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} \frac{(\sigma_{i}^{2} - \sigma_{j}^{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}}\, \Omega_{ij}^{2} \,} with Ω=R⊤dR∈𝔰𝔬(n)\Omega = R^{\top} dR \in \mathfrak{so}(n).

The metric is easier to interpret in this form. The first two terms correspond to the metric of the univariate Gaussian, summed over each of the coordinates associated with the eigenvectors that form the new basis. The third term, which couples the different standard deviations, corresponds to a change in the orientation of the Gaussian. Let us examine this in more detail.

4.2.4 Change in the mean

If we assume dΣ=0d\Sigma = 0, the metric reduces to gμ,Σ(dμ,dμ)=dμ⊤Σ−1dμ.g_{\mu, \Sigma}(d\mu, d\mu) = d\mu^{\top} \Sigma^{-1} d\mu. This is the squared Mahalanobis norm of dμd\mu associated with Σ\Sigma (figure 16). Some care is nevertheless required: this quadratic form describes an infinitesimal displacement at fixed Σ\Sigma, not a distance between two Gaussians, and it does not imply that geodesics of the full model keep Σ\Sigma constant. As in the univariate case, the shortest path between two Gaussians with the same covariance but different means involves spreading the covariance along the way (figures 17–19).

4.2.5 Change in standard deviation

If only the standard deviations are allowed to vary (dμ=0d\mu = 0, dR=0dR = 0), the metric reduces to ds2=2∑i=1ndσi2σi2.ds^{2} = 2\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{d\sigma_{i}^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}. Consider the path between two centred Gaussians with the same orientation θ=π/4\theta = \pi/4, whose principal variances change from (1,4)(1,\ 4) to (4,1)(4,\ 1), that is, with (σ1,σ2)(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}) moving from (1,2)(1,\ 2) to (2,1)(2,\ 1). This time, μ\mu and θ\theta remain fixed along the geodesic; only the pair (σ1,σ2)(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}) changes. In the plane of standard deviations, the path is not the Euclidean line segment: it is the arc σ1(t)=2t\sigma_{1}(t) = 2^{t}, σ2(t)=21−t\sigma_{2}(t) = 2^{1-t} (figure 20), and the densities gradually exchange their elongations (figure 21).

4.2.6 Change in orientation

Finally, if only the orientation is allowed to vary (dμ=0d\mu = 0, dσi=0d\sigma_{i} = 0), only the third term remains: ds2=∑1≤i<j≤n(σi2−σj2)2σi2σj2Ωij2.ds^{2} = \sum_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} \frac{(\sigma_{i}^{2} - \sigma_{j}^{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}\sigma_{j}^{2}}\, \Omega_{ij}^{2}. We start from a centred Gaussian with elongation (1,4)(1,\ 4), oriented at π/4\pi/4, and take it to 3π/43\pi/4 without changing either μ\mu or the σi\sigma_{i}. The densities rotate in place (figure 22); the graph of θ(t)\theta(t) is a straight line, and the Riemannian speed gθθ|θ̇|\sqrt{g_{\theta\theta}}\,|\dot\theta| remains constant (figure 23).

4.2.7 When everything moves together

The three previous motions combine as soon as two arbitrary Gaussians are connected. The mean, standard deviations, and orientation then move together, each at the rate dictated by its part of the metric. Eriksen [15] integrates geodesics with a prescribed initial velocity using the exponential of a (2n+1)×(2n+1)(2n+1)\times(2n+1) matrix; Calvo and Oller [16] give an explicit form. Connecting two Gaussians is a boundary-value problem: once n≥2n\ge 2, there is no elementary formula for either the path or the distance. Here we approximate this boundary-value problem by minimizing a discrete Riemannian energy (section 9). As an illustration, we connect 𝒩((0,0),Σ1)to𝒩((2,2),Σ2),\mathcal{N}\bigl((0,0),\Sigma_{1}\bigr) \quad\text{to}\quad \mathcal{N}\bigl((2,2),\Sigma_{2}\bigr), where Σ1\Sigma_{1} has elongation (1,4)(1,\ 4) and orientation π/4\pi/4, while Σ2\Sigma_{2} has elongation (4,1)(4,\ 1) and orientation 3π/43\pi/4. Figure 24 shows the densities along the geodesic: the mass shifts, stretches, and rotates at the same time. Halfway along the path, the two standard deviations draw closer; the Gaussian becomes almost isotropic and gθθg_{\theta\theta} collapses, allowing the orientation to rotate at little cost before closing onto the target ellipse. This change can be seen clearly in the space (σ1,σ2,θ)(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\theta) (figure 25): the path rises in θ\theta mainly when σ1≈σ2\sigma_{1}\approx\sigma_{2}.

The pullback metric follows: gθ(dθ1,dθ2)=∫Edpθ(dθ1)dpθ(dθ2)pθdx=𝔼θ[(ϕ(X)−𝔼θϕ)⊤dθ1(ϕ(X)−𝔼θϕ)⊤dθ2]=dθ1⊤Covθ(ϕ(X))dθ2.\begin{aligned} g_{\theta}(d\theta_{1}, d\theta_{2}) &= \int_{E} \frac{dp_{\theta}(d\theta_{1})\, dp_{\theta}(d\theta_{2})}{p_{\theta}}\, dx \\ &= \mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left[ \bigl(\phi(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\phi\bigr)^{\top} d\theta_{1}\; \bigl(\phi(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\phi\bigr)^{\top} d\theta_{2} \right] \\ &= d\theta_{1}^{\top}\, \mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}\bigl(\phi(X)\bigr)\, d\theta_{2}. \end{aligned} The Fisher–Rao geometry on the parameter space is therefore entirely encoded in Covθ(ϕ(X))\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}(\phi(X)), the covariance of ϕ(X)\phi(X) when XX has density pθp_{\theta}.

By construction, Covθ(ϕ(X))\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}(\phi(X)) is a symmetric positive-semidefinite matrix. It is positive definite when the components of ϕ\phi are affinely independent, that is, when no non-trivial linear combination v⊤ϕ(x)v^{\top}\phi(x) is constant almost everywhere. In other words, the image of ϕ\phi in ℝk\mathbb{R}^{k} must not be contained in a proper affine subspace. The example ϕ(x)=(x,x2)\phi(x)=(x,x^{2}) traces a parabola, and the condition is satisfied; with ϕ(x)=(x,5x)\phi(x)=(x,5x), the image is a line and the covariance is degenerate.

This shows the double role of ϕ\phi: it determines the random variable whose covariance is taken, and it enters into pθp_{\theta}, hence into the law under which this covariance is computed.

It is also the Hessian of log⁡Zθ\log Z_{\theta}: gθ=Hess⁡θlog⁡Zθ=Covθ(ϕ(X))\boxed{\, g_{\theta} = \mathop{\mathrm{Hess}}_{\theta}\log Z_{\theta} = \mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}\bigl(\phi(X)\bigr) \,} In general, a Riemannian metric amounts to choosing an inner product at every point: k(k+1)/2k(k+1)/2 functions of θ\theta. Here everything comes from a single convex function, log⁡Zθ\log Z_{\theta}. We no longer choose gg directly: we choose a potential, and gg is its Hessian.

The total symmetry of ∂kgij\partial_{k}g_{ij} follows immediately. These are the third derivatives of log⁡Zθ\log Z_{\theta}, and Schwarz’s theorem makes them independent of the order of the indices. Conversely, in affine coordinates, g=Hessψg=\mathrm{Hess}\,\psi if and only if ∂kgij=∂igkj=∂jgik\partial_{k} g_{ij} = \partial_{i} g_{kj} = \partial_{j} g_{ik} everywhere. This is the integrability condition, sometimes called the Codazzi condition: the way g(ei,ej)g(e_{i},e_{j}) changes when moving along eke_{k} does not depend on which index plays which role. A general metric need not integrate to a potential.

This does not prevent the eigenaxes from rotating. For ψ=cosh⁡θ1+cosh⁡θ2+0,3θ1θ2\psi=\cosh\theta_{1}+\cosh\theta_{2}+0{,}3\,\theta_{1}\theta_{2}, the angle φ\varphi of the eigenvectors satisfies tan⁡(2φ)=0,6/(cosh⁡θ1−cosh⁡θ2)\tan(2\varphi)=0{,}6/(\cosh\theta_{1}-\cosh\theta_{2}): the inclination varies with θ\theta. What is ruled out is a field gg for which the ∂kgij\partial_{k}g_{ij} are not totally symmetric.

Figure 34 illustrates this in the (θ1,θ2)(\theta_{1},\theta_{2}) plane. The ellipses are circles of the metric: the vectors vv such that gθ(v,v)=r2g_{\theta}(v,v)=r^{2}, with rr slightly smaller than 11 so that the field fits on the grid. The background shows ∂2g11−∂1g12\partial_{2}g_{11}-\partial_{1}g_{12}, one component of the obstruction. It is non-zero: this field does not come from any potential.

This rigidity has a counterpart. The gradient of the potential provides a second coordinate system, dual to the first: η=−∇θlog⁡Zθ=𝔼θ[ϕ].\eta = -\nabla_{\theta}\log Z_{\theta} = \mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\phi]. In these coordinates, g−1g^{-1} is the Hessian of the Legendre potential. We therefore have two affine charts, and the straight lines in each are distinguished paths of the dual Hessian geometry; they are not the Riemannian geodesics of gg. This is the intuitive content of the dually flat geometry of exponential families [17], [18]. For Hessian geometry in general, see Shima [20]; for what this constraint rules out among Riemannian metrics, see Amari–Armstrong [21].

6.3 Analysis of the metric

The formula gθ(dθ,dθ)=Varθ(ϕ(X)⊤dθ)g_{\theta}(d\theta, d\theta) = \mathrm{Var}_{\theta}\bigl(\phi(X)^{\top} d\theta\bigr) reads as follows. A displacement dθd\theta is costly when the combination ϕ⊤dθ\phi^{\top}d\theta fluctuates a lot under pθp_{\theta}, and cheap when it is almost constant over the xx that the law gives weight to. This is the reading of section 3.2, brought back onto Θ\Theta: dpθ(dθ)/pθ=−(ϕ−𝔼θϕ)⊤dθdp_{\theta}(d\theta)/p_{\theta} = -\bigl(\phi-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\phi\bigr)^{\top}d\theta, so the ratio u/pu/p is none other than the centred energy gap. Fisher–Rao pays for these fluctuations, not the mean level, which ZθZ_{\theta} absorbs.

Concretely, ϕ\phi chooses what θ\theta is allowed to see. Two regions of EE that ϕ\phi does not distinguish remain on an equal footing, whatever the parameters. With ϕ(x)=x\phi(x)=x, one can only tilt the density linearly; ϕ(x)=(x,x2)\phi(x)=(x,x^{2}) adds control of the spread; ϕ(x)=cos⁡x\phi(x)=\cos x sees only one oscillation, and a bounded one. Since ϕ\phi also enters pθp_{\theta}, the effective support moves with θ\theta: increasing θi\theta_{i} penalizes the xx where ϕi\phi_{i} is large, the mass concentrates elsewhere, and Covθ(ϕ)\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}(\phi) changes. The metric is not constant on Θ\Theta.

When pθp_{\theta} spreads over a wide range of values of ϕ\phi, each step of θ\theta deforms the density a great deal. When it concentrates, typically for |θ||\theta| large, ϕ(X)\phi(X) freezes near a minimizer of ϕ⊤θ\phi^{\top}\theta, the variance drops, and the same dθd\theta in natural coordinates costs less and less. The case ϕ(x)=x\phi(x)=x on ℝ+\mathbb{R}_{+}, the exponential law, makes this readable: gθ=1/θ2g_{\theta}=1/\theta^{2}, which blows up as θ→0\theta\to 0 (the density flattens out) and dies out as θ→+∞\theta\to+\infty (all the mass is already glued to the origin).

On unbounded EE, this blow-up often occurs at the boundary of the natural domain Θ={θ:Zθ<∞}\Theta=\{\theta : Z_{\theta}<\infty\}. For ϕ(x)=x2\phi(x)=x^{2} on ℝ\mathbb{R}, ZθZ_{\theta} is finite only if θ>0\theta>0; as θ↓0\theta\downarrow 0, the density spreads, and Varθ(X2)\mathrm{Var}_{\theta}(X^{2}) goes to infinity. This is σ→+∞\sigma\to+\infty in the Gaussian, read here in the natural parameter. The figures in this section, for their part, normalize on a bounded piece of EE: ZθZ_{\theta} stays finite, the metric does not go to infinity. What remains are the anisotropies and the near-degeneracies.

If the image of ϕ\phi is almost contained in an affine subspace, an eigenvalue of Covθ(ϕ)\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}(\phi) is tiny: the corresponding direction of θ\theta adds almost a constant to the energy, and so hardly changes pθp_{\theta}. This is the case of ϕ(x)=(x2,cos⁡x)\phi(x)=(x^{2},\cos x) on a short interval, where cos⁡x≈1−x2/2\cos x \approx 1 - x^{2}/2. Conversely, ϕ(x)=(x,x2)\phi(x)=(x,x^{2}) traces a parabola, the two directions remain visible, and moving θ\theta costs in the whole plane. Figure 35 makes this progressive degeneration visible in a one-parameter family of feature maps. Figure 36 shows, at a fixed θ⋆\theta_{\star}, what a step of the same length in each of the two eigen-directions does to the density: along the direction of the smallest eigenvalue, where the metric is nearly degenerate, the density barely moves, whereas along the direction of the largest eigenvalue the same step deforms it markedly. The anisotropy is thus read directly on the displacement function dpθ(dθ)\mathrm{d}p_{\theta}(\mathrm{d}\theta).

Because gθg_{\theta} varies with θ\theta, the shortest paths in Θ\Theta are not Euclidean segments. How they bend is read on the derivatives of the metric. Since gij=∂i∂jlog⁡Zθg_{ij}=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\log Z_{\theta} is a Hessian, these derivatives are totally symmetric, and ∂i∂j∂klog⁡Zθ=−𝔼θ[(ϕi−𝔼θϕi)(ϕj−𝔼θϕj)(ϕk−𝔼θϕk)].\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\log Z_{\theta} = - \mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left[ \bigl(\phi_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\phi_{i}\bigr) \bigl(\phi_{j}-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\phi_{j}\bigr) \bigl(\phi_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\phi_{k}\bigr) \right]. When this third moment is large, Covθ(ϕ)\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}(\phi) changes quickly along a path, and the geodesic leaves the Euclidean chord.

7.3 Analysis of the metric

The previous formula reads directly. For dθ∈TθΘ≃ℝkd\theta\in T_{\theta}\Theta\simeq\mathbb{R}^{k}, gθ(dθ,dθ)=Varθ(∇θU(θ,X)⊤dθ).g_{\theta}(d\theta,d\theta) = \mathrm{Var}_{\theta}\bigl( \nabla_{\theta}U(\theta,X)^{\top} d\theta \bigr). The cost of an infinitesimal displacement dθd\theta is therefore the variance, under the current law pθp_{\theta}, of the energy modification it produces. A parameter direction is costly when it modifies the energy very differently depending on xx; it costs little when this modification stays almost constant over the states that pθp_{\theta} gives weight to.

That this is a variance, and not a second-order moment, has a simple reading. Adding to the energy a quantity independent of xx does not change the density: it is absorbed by the normalization constant. The metric therefore sees only the fluctuations of the energy variation around its mean. In particular, a direction dθd\theta such that ∇θU(θ,x)⊤dθ=c\nabla_{\theta}U(\theta,x)^{\top} d\theta = c for almost every xx, with cc independent of xx, has zero cost: to first order, it only adds a constant to the energy and does not move pθp_{\theta} in the space of densities.

We also specify when the pullback actually defines a Riemannian metric. The matrix Covθ(∇θU(θ,X))\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}U(\theta,X)\bigr) is always symmetric positive semidefinite, as soon as the second-order moments exist. It is positive definite if and only if ∀dθ≠0,Varθ(∇θU(θ,X)⊤dθ)>0,\forall\, d\theta\neq 0,\qquad \mathrm{Var}_{\theta}\bigl( \nabla_{\theta}U(\theta,X)^{\top} d\theta \bigr) > 0, that is, if no non-trivial linear combination of the components of ∇θU(θ,⋅)\nabla_{\theta}U(\theta,\cdot) is constant almost everywhere. This is exactly the injectivity of dpθ:TθΘ→Tpθ𝒫+(E).dp_{\theta} \colon T_{\theta}\Theta \longrightarrow T_{p_{\theta}}\mathcal{P}_{+}(E). In other words, the covariance is positive definite precisely when θ↦pθ\theta\mapsto p_{\theta} is an immersion at θ\theta. A degeneracy reflects a redundancy of the parametrization: there exists a non-zero direction in the parameter space that does not modify the distribution to first order.

The passage from the linear case to the non-linear case can also be read in the normalization constant. We have ∇θlog⁡Zθ=−𝔼θ[∇θU(θ,X)],\nabla_{\theta}\log Z_{\theta} = -\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[\nabla_{\theta}U(\theta,X)\bigr], and, differentiating a second time, Hess⁡θlog⁡Zθ=Covθ(∇θU(θ,X))−𝔼θ[Hess⁡θU(θ,X)].\mathop{\mathrm{Hess}}_{\theta}\log Z_{\theta} = \mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}U(\theta,X)\bigr) - \mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[\mathop{\mathrm{Hess}}_{\theta}U(\theta,X)\bigr]. The Fisher matrix therefore reads Covθ(∇θU(θ,X))=Hess⁡θlog⁡Zθ+𝔼θ[Hess⁡θU(θ,X)].\boxed{ \mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}U(\theta,X)\bigr) = \mathop{\mathrm{Hess}}_{\theta}\log Z_{\theta} + \mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[\mathop{\mathrm{Hess}}_{\theta}U(\theta,X)\bigr] }. In the linear family U(θ,x)=ϕ(x)⊤θU(\theta,x)=\phi(x)^{\top}\theta, the second term vanishes and we recover the Hessian metric Covθ(ϕ(X))=Hess⁡θlog⁡Zθ.\mathrm{Cov}_{\theta}\bigl(\phi(X)\bigr) = \mathop{\mathrm{Hess}}_{\theta}\log Z_{\theta}. In the non-linear case, the covariance remains the fundamental quantity, but the metric is no longer in general the Hessian of log⁡Zθ\log Z_{\theta} alone: an additional term appears, which measures the curvature of the energy with respect to the parameters.

8 Push-forward family

For this last family, we reverse the point of view. So far the family was given by a formula for the density, and θ\theta tuned this formula. We now fix a base law once and for all, and it is the observation space that gets deformed.

8.1 Differential

The computation below is the one used for the Reynolds transport theorem: we work in the initial coordinates, we write that the probability carried by a transported region does not change, and we differentiate.

8.1.1 Notation for the two variables

Two variables are in play, the parameter and the point of EE, and the whole computation consists in keeping track of which one is being differentiated. We therefore regard the transport as a function of both, ϕ:Θ×E→E,(θ,x)↦ϕ(θ,x)=ϕθ(x),\phi \colon \Theta \times E \to E, \qquad (\theta, x) \longmapsto \phi(\theta, x) = \phi_{\theta}(x), where xx is the point read by qq and y=ϕ(θ,x)y = \phi(\theta, x) the point read by pθp_{\theta}. 8.3 Analysis of the metric

The divergence form says what the metric looks at. Fisher–Rao does not see vdθv_{d\theta}, but div⁡(pθvdθ)\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_{\theta}v_{d\theta}): what costs is the local compression or dilation of mass, not the displacement itself. A direction dθd\theta whose velocity field satisfies div⁡(pθvdθ)=0\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\bigl(p_{\theta}\, v_{d\theta}\bigr) = 0 therefore costs nothing. The flux pθvdθp_{\theta}v_{d\theta} then circulates without creating or destroying mass anywhere: the points of EE move, the density does not. Figure 44 shows a concrete instance: the isotropic Gaussian p=𝒩(0,I2)p=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{2}) and the rotational field v(y)=(−y2,y1)v(y)=(-y_{2},\, y_{1}). Then div⁡v=0\mathop{\mathrm{div}}v=0 and ⟨∇p,v⟩=0\left\langle \nabla p,\, v \right\rangle=0, hence div⁡(pv)=0\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(pv)=0 and u=−div⁡(pv)=0u=-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(pv)=0.

These free directions are not an accident of the choice of examples. If σ:E→E\sigma \colon E \to E preserves qq, that is σ♯q=q\sigma_{\sharp}q = q, then ϕθ\phi_{\theta} and ϕθ∘σ\phi_{\theta}\circ\sigma give the same pθp_{\theta}. The parametrization by transports is therefore massively redundant, and this redundancy must be broken for the metric to be positive definite. We already said it for the nonlinear Gibbs family: gθg_{\theta} is positive definite exactly when θ↦pθ\theta \mapsto p_{\theta} is an immersion at θ\theta. Here, the source of the degeneracy has a name: the rearrangements that preserve the base. The rotation of figure 44 is exactly such a rearrangement of the isotropic Gaussian.

This quotient viewpoint is closely related to the geometric description of spaces of densities through diffeomorphism groups; see Khesin, Lenells, Misiołek, and Preston [6].

The affine case serves as a check. For a mean displacement, vdμ=dμv_{d\mu} = d\mu is constant, its divergence is zero, hence ξdμ=⟨∇logpθ,dμ⟩\xi_{d\mu} = \left\langle \nabla\log p_{\theta},\, d\mu \right\rangle and gθ(dμ,dμ)=dμ⊤𝔼θ[∇log⁡pθ∇log⁡pθ⊤]dμ=dμ⊤Σ−1dμg_{\theta}(d\mu, d\mu) = d\mu^{\top}\, \mathbb{E}_{\theta}\bigl[\nabla\log p_{\theta}\, \nabla\log p_{\theta}^{\top}\bigr]\, d\mu = d\mu^{\top}\Sigma^{-1} d\mu for the Gaussian: the Mahalanobis distance of the Gaussian chapter, recovered without computing an integral. The dispersion block reads the same way, with the linear field v(y)=dAA−1(y−μ)v(y) = dA\,A^{-1}(y-\mu), of constant divergence tr⁡(dAA−1)\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(dA\,A^{-1}).

The identity ∂ilog⁡pθ=−ξi\partial_{i}\log p_{\theta} = -\xi_{i} deserves a pause: the score is nothing other than the local rate of mass compression produced by θi\theta_{i}. Where the transport compresses, the log-density rises; where it dilates, it falls.

We had opposed Fisher–Rao and Wasserstein in section 3.2. The tangent is u=−div⁡(pv)u=-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(pv), and several vv can realize it. 10 Conclusion

The construction has been the same throughout the different families considered in this paper. A parametric model is represented by the map θ↦pθ∈𝒫+(E),\theta \longmapsto p_\theta \in \mathcal{P}_{+}(E), and the parameter space is equipped with the metric obtained by pulling back the Fisher–Rao metric gFRg^{\mathrm{FR}} along this map. Whenever the parametrization is an immersion, this pullback defines a Riemannian metric on the parameter space. The geometry is therefore not imposed directly on the parameters: it is inherited from the way in which variations of θ\theta deform the corresponding probability density.

At the infinitesimal level, the interpretation of Fisher–Rao has remained the same in all the examples. A small parameter displacement dθ∈TθΘd\theta\in T_\theta\Theta produces a density variation dpθ(dθ)dp_\theta(d\theta), whose squared cost is gθ(dθ,dθ)=∫E(dpθ(dθ)(x)pθ(x))2pθ(x)dx.g_\theta(d\theta,d\theta) = \int_E \left( \frac{dp_\theta(d\theta)(x)}{p_\theta(x)} \right)^2 p_\theta(x)\,dx. Thus Fisher–Rao measures the relative variation of the density produced by the parameter displacement. In the interpretation developed throughout this paper, this corresponds to a local loss of overlap: the same absolute variation is more costly where the density is small than where most of the probability mass is concentrated. The pullback translates this infinitesimal cost into the coordinates of the parameter space.

The resulting geodesics, however, depend not only on the local expression of the metric, but also on the parametric family itself. A path in Θ\Theta gives a path of densities constrained to remain in {pθ:θ∈Θ}⊂𝒫+(E).\{p_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}\subset\mathcal{P}_{+}(E). The shortest Fisher–Rao path between two densities in the ambient space 𝒫+(E)\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) may leave this subset and therefore need not correspond to an admissible path in the statistical model. The intrinsic geodesics considered here must instead follow the deformations allowed by the parametrization. In this sense, the geometry of each family reflects both the ambient Fisher–Rao metric and the particular way in which the family is immersed in the space of probability densities.

This common construction takes different forms in the families studied above. For the Gaussian family, the metric separates the effects of location and covariance. In the univariate case, it is, up to scale, the hyperbolic metric of the Poincaré half-plane: translating a sharply concentrated Gaussian is more costly than translating a diffuse one, and a geodesic may increase the variance before changing the mean. In higher dimension, mean displacements are measured through the Mahalanobis quadratic form, while the covariance block distinguishes changes of scale, anisotropy, and orientation.

The elliptical Laplace family exhibits a closely related decomposition. Location and orientation have the same geometric structure as in the Gaussian case, up to dimension-dependent factors, whereas the dispersion block contains an additional coupling between the principal scales. Similar endpoint configurations may therefore be connected through different intermediate deformations.

For a linear Gibbs family, pθ(x)=1Zθexp⁡(−ϕ(x)⊤θ),p_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{Z_\theta} \exp\!\bigl(-\phi(x)^\top\theta\bigr), the metric takes the particularly rigid form gθ=Cov⁡θ(ϕ(X))=Hess⁡θlog⁡Zθ.g_\theta = \operatorname{Cov}_\theta\!\bigl(\phi(X)\bigr) = \mathop{\mathrm{Hess}}_\theta \log Z_\theta. The whole metric is thus generated by the single convex potential log⁡Zθ\log Z_\theta. Its anisotropy records the fluctuations of the features ϕ(X)\phi(X) under the current distribution, and degeneracy appears when some parameter direction produces an almost constant variation of the energy.

When the Gibbs energy depends nonlinearly on the parameters, pθ(x)=1Zθe−U(θ,x),p_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{Z_\theta}e^{-U(\theta,x)}, the covariance interpretation remains: gθ=Cov⁡θ(∇θU(θ,X)).g_\theta = \operatorname{Cov}_\theta \bigl(\nabla_\theta U(\theta,X)\bigr). In general, however, the metric is no longer the Hessian of log⁡Zθ\log Z_\theta alone. The additional term involving the curvature of UU with respect to θ\theta marks the loss of the Hessian structure specific to the linear family.

Finally, the push-forward construction gives a complementary interpretation. If pθ=(φθ)♯qp_\theta=(\varphi_\theta)_\sharp q and a parameter displacement dθd\theta induces the velocity field vdθv_{d\theta} on EE, then the corresponding density variation satisfies dpθ(dθ)=−div⁡x(pθvdθ).dp_\theta(d\theta) = -\mathop{\mathrm{div}}_x\!\bigl(p_\theta v_{d\theta}\bigr). Consequently, dpθ(dθ)pθ=−div⁡x(pθvdθ)pθ.\frac{dp_\theta(d\theta)}{p_\theta} = -\frac{ \mathop{\mathrm{div}}_x\!\bigl(p_\theta v_{d\theta}\bigr) }{ p_\theta }. In this representation, Fisher–Rao does not measure the magnitude of the displacement field itself. What it sees is the local compression or dilation of probability mass generated by that field. A motion that rearranges points while leaving the density unchanged is therefore invisible to Fisher–Rao.

This last formulation also provides a natural bridge with Wasserstein–Otto geometry. The same continuity equation appears there, but it is used to define a different infinitesimal cost. Given a tangent density variation uu, with ∫Eudx=0,\int_E u\,dx=0, one considers all velocity fields vv satisfying u+div⁡x(pv)=0.u+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}_x(pv)=0. The Wasserstein–Otto norm is then ‖u‖W2,p2=infvu+div⁡x(pv)=0∫E‖v(x)‖2p(x)dx.\lVert u\rVert_{W_2,p}^{2} = \inf_{\substack{v\\u+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}_x(pv)=0}} \int_E \lVert v(x)\rVert^{2}p(x)\,dx. Under the usual assumptions, the minimizing velocity field can be taken to be a gradient field, and the associated Riemannian distance is the quadratic Wasserstein distance W2W_2 [8], [7].

The contrast with Fisher–Rao is particularly transparent at the infinitesimal level. For the same tangent density variation uu, Fisher–Rao assigns the cost ‖u‖FR,p2=∫Eu(x)2p(x)dx,\lVert u\rVert_{\mathrm{FR},p}^{2} = \int_E \frac{u(x)^2}{p(x)}\,dx, whereas Wasserstein asks for the least kinetic energy required to realize uu through a displacement of mass. Fisher–Rao measures the amplitude of a relative change of density; Wasserstein measures how mass must move through the geometry of the observation space. The former is insensitive to a transport that leaves the density unchanged, while the latter is built from the displacement field itself.

It would therefore be natural to repeat the construction of this atlas with the Wasserstein–Otto metric: pull it back along the same parametric families, derive the corresponding metrics on their parameter spaces, and compare the resulting geodesics with those obtained here. Such a comparison would place the two geometries on exactly the same models and make explicit how statistical variation and spatial transport assign different costs to the same parameter displacement.

The purpose of the present atlas has been to keep the ambient Fisher–Rao geometry fixed and to vary the family through which it is observed. The examples show how this single infinitesimal notion of statistical discrepancy produces markedly different finite-dimensional geometries once pulled back to parameter space. The metric thus records how a model turns parameter variations into changes of probability law, while its geodesics describe the least costly statistical deformations that remain available within that model.
